Gold crowns cost more upfront but can last several decades and resist chipping exceptionally well, making them a strong choice for back molars where looks matter less. Porcelain crowns cost less initially, wear out faster (typically 10-15 years), but match the natural color and translucency of your teeth — the better choice for front teeth and anywhere your smile shows. Most patients choose based on which tooth is being restored and how visible it is.

Not all
[dental crowns](/crowns) are the same, and the material you choose affects how long it lasts, what it costs, and how it looks. Gold and porcelain sit at opposite ends of that tradeoff, so here's how Dr. Isaiah Davis and our Columbia, SC team help patients decide between them.

## Durability

Gold is exceptionally strong and highly resistant to chipping and wear, which is why it's held up in dentistry for over a century — particularly for molars, where chewing forces are highest. Porcelain is durable too, but more susceptible to wear and chipping over time, especially under heavy bite forces.

## Appearance

This is where the two materials diverge the most. Gold crowns are metallic and visible — they simply don't blend in the way a tooth-colored option does, which is why they're rarely chosen for front teeth. Porcelain crowns mimic the natural color and translucency of real enamel, making them the preferred choice anywhere your smile is visible when you talk or laugh.

## Longevity

Gold crowns can last several decades, often outliving porcelain by a wide margin with proper care. Porcelain crowns typically last 10-15 years, depending on your bite, habits, and how well you maintain them.

## Maintenance and Biocompatibility

Gold requires comparatively little upkeep once placed and is highly biocompatible, with a long track record of causing minimal gum irritation. Porcelain is also biocompatible and, beyond its cosmetic appeal, requires the same care as your natural teeth — regular brushing, flossing, and checkups.

## Cost and Insurance

Gold crowns generally carry a higher material cost upfront. Porcelain is often more accessible initially, though its shorter lifespan can mean replacement costs down the line. Insurance coverage varies significantly by plan and by which tooth is being treated, so it's worth confirming your specific benefits before deciding.

## Which One Should You Choose?

**Choose gold if:** the crown is on a back molar, longevity and strength matter more to you than appearance, and you don't mind the metallic look on a tooth that isn't visible when you smile.

**Choose porcelain if:** the crown is on a front tooth or anywhere visible, and a natural appearance is a priority — even knowing it may need replacement sooner than gold.
